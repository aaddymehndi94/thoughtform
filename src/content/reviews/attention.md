# Simone Weil — research and teaching review
Original30 sketch cards and sources preserved in src/content/research. These20 cards replace the selected sketches with complete reading bodies, stage-based lessons, and two explanatory stills each. All dialogue is an original illustrative example. Notebook references are posthumous selections, not a finished systematic treatise. Rendered approval belongs to the main integration review.

## weil-attention
Teaching point: A ready answer can get in the way of a real person.
Alternatives: reply balloons crowd a conversation; geometry page obscured by a clenched hand; the actual person behind a prepared case label.
Chosen progression: A friend begins. You already have an answer. → Your ready-made advice takes up the space for listening. → Let the answer wait. A new detail can arrive. → Attention lets the person change your picture.
Still1: Knowledge stays nearby without covering the problem.
Still2: An open question gives an unexpected need room to appear.
Qualification: For Weil, this receptivity belongs to her religious account of prayer and love; it is more demanding than a trick for concentrating. The person or problem exists beyond your wish to look useful, clever, or kind. You might ask a quieter question, hear a detail you missed, then offer help that fits. Attention can lead to action. Its first movement is letting reality alter your picture, rather than making reality perform the role you had prepared for it.

## weil-error
Teaching point: A mistake can teach you something your excuses keep hidden.
Alternatives: corrected arithmetic inspected line by line; folded report reopened at the faulty assumption; a crooked joint compared against its measurements.
Chosen progression: The mark feels like a judgment on your whole ability. → Turn back to the particular line instead of turning the page over. → The fault has a location: this division. → Correction turns a verdict into something you can work on.
Still1: A missed observation and a global self-judgment are different things.
Still2: Inspecting the error restores a specific next action.
Qualification: Weil describes this discipline in the severe language of humility and spiritual formation. It should not be turned into an invitation to humiliate students, or to treat every struggle as stupidity. Good teaching, rest, and clear instructions matter. The useful distinction is between inspecting a piece of work and protecting a flattering picture of its author. Once the failed step can be seen, the next attempt becomes more precise. The page changes from a verdict into something you can learn from.

## weil-neighbour
Teaching point: Someone in pain needs room to be more than your idea of their pain.
Alternatives: service desk with a form moved aside; patient and doctor comparing a treatment plan; food queue where one person is seen beyond their ticket.
Chosen progression: A familiar category can make the person disappear. → Their situation includes a detail the form does not contain. → Put the category beside the person. Ask what is happening. → Recognition helps the practical response fit this life.
Still1: A case category leaves out parts of the actual situation.
Still2: Care keeps the recipient able to correct the plan.
Qualification: This is part of Weil’s Christian account of love and justice. She gives attentive recognition an unusually deep moral significance; it is not merely good customer service. Listening alone cannot supply food, safety, or legal help, and urgent need may require prompt action. But those actions can still be guided by the person who needs them. A helpful plan becomes better when the recipient can correct it. Care loses something when being generous matters more than learning what the other person is enduring.

## weil-affliction
Teaching point: Some suffering damages the place a person has in the world.
Alternatives: supports of a working life damaged together; a person losing name badges while remaining present; three kinds of harm shown intersecting.
Chosen progression: A life rests on several supports. → An injury brings pain and interrupts work. → Money, belonging, and a sense of place may break together. → Affliction reaches through the whole life. The person still matters.
Still1: Physical, social, and inner harm can intersect.
Still2: A person’s silence may express damage rather than absence of need.
Qualification: Her account is philosophical and theological, not a clinical scale for measuring distress. It should not be used to decide whose suffering counts, or to demand that another person find spiritual value in catastrophe. Weil’s severity asks something of the witness: resist treating the injured person as a cautionary tale, a burden, or a failed version of yourself. Practical support matters because a life has material supports. Patient recognition matters because the person remains someone, even when their former place is gone.

## weil-force
Teaching point: Force can silence a person before it destroys their body.
Alternatives: captive wishes suppressed by a guard’s command; warrior overtaken by changing terrain; a person’s voice flattened into an administrative token.
Chosen progression: The captive has wishes. The guard has the weapon. → A threat makes the next movement depend on another person. → The person is alive, but treated as something to command. → Power can change hands. Force gives no one permanent safety.
Still1: Death is the literal limit; coercion begins the reduction while a person lives.
Still2: The wielder’s confidence exceeds the limited power of the weapon.
Qualification: This is Weil’s interpretation of an ancient poem, not a claim that every conflict works in exactly the same way. A harsh disagreement differs from captivity; the analogy should not erase the difference. Her central question travels further: where does power make another person’s wishes disappear from consideration? Resistance, protection, and institutions matter because recognizing someone’s humanity is fragile when violence governs the room. Seeing the person must become more than a kindly feeling in the observer.

## weil-gravity
Teaching point: A humiliation can become someone else’s burden.
Alternatives: workplace hurt travels down stepped ground; one harsh sentence passed through a queue; an inherited weight is set down beside a complaint.
Chosen progression: The worker receives an injury they cannot safely answer. → The pressure travels with the worker. → A stranger becomes the easier outlet. → Seeing the source can interrupt the next transfer.
Still1: A chain of reactions is different from responsibility for the original hurt.
Still2: The original injustice can be addressed without passing it onward.
Qualification: Weil places this image beside grace, her religious name for a goodness that interrupts ordinary moral necessity. In an everyday example, the interruption might begin when a person notices the borrowed anger and refuses to spend it on a stranger. That does not settle the original injustice. They may still need help, a boundary, or a complaint. The small change is that an available weaker person stops being the outlet. Acknowledging the source of pressure can make its next destination less automatic.

## weil-compensation
Teaching point: Feeling diminished can make you look for someone to diminish.
Alternatives: failed dinner joke paid for by a correction; empty applause recovered in an imaginary theatre; a slight and a comfort distinguished from revenge.
Chosen progression: Your story falls flat. A small loss of status hurts. → The friend makes a harmless mistake. → A triumphant correction recruits their embarrassment as payment. → The first discomfort can pass without making the friend pay.
Still1: Comfort addresses a hurt; humiliating an uninvolved person shifts it.
Still2: An imagined revenge can restore status while leaving the original hurt unexplained.
Qualification: Ordinary consolation and fair repair have their place. Being comforted after a loss differs from making an uninvolved person pay for it. Weil’s religious concern is the mind’s unwillingness to remain with a deprivation when no rightful payment is available. In the dinner example, the discomfort can be allowed to pass without staging another person’s embarrassment. The joke failed; a whole identity has not failed with it. That modest recognition leaves the room less crowded by the need to recover yourself.

## weil-imagination
Teaching point: A comforting explanation can replace the thing you need to understand.
Alternatives: unanswered phone message covered by an accusation; unfilled theatre populated with a flattering plot; evidence notebook separated from a hypothesis.
Chosen progression: A message has no reply yet. The cause is unknown. → An accusation supplies an answer before the facts arrive. → Now the next message is read through the invented story. → Keep the guess available to correction.
Still1: A guess can stay tentative instead of becoming a private verdict.
Still2: Imagination used for play leaves room for reality to remain different.
Qualification: Her criticism does not require abandoning novels, images, planning, or creative play. It concerns fantasy that prevents contact with what is real. In an ordinary friendship, you can ask, wait, or look at the wider pattern without promoting a guess into a fact. You may eventually discover something painful; openness is not a guarantee of a pleasant answer. It gives the other person, and the evidence, a chance to exist outside the explanation your hurt has already written for them.

## weil-detachment
Teaching point: Caring for something need not mean making it yours.
Alternatives: a gift held in a closing hand then offered openly; plant tended without forcing its shape; a promise distinguished from an imagined return.
Chosen progression: The gift is good. Your hoped-for response becomes a hidden contract. → A tight grip makes the gift answer to the giver. → Loosen the claim while keeping the care. → The recipient can have a response of their own.
Still1: Detachment loosens a demand about the outcome, not a responsibility to care.
Still2: A promise is a genuine duty; an imagined return is a different claim.
Qualification: Detachment should not excuse neglect or ask people to tolerate mistreatment. Promises and responsibilities still matter, and a hurt relationship may need an honest conversation. The useful question concerns ownership of the outcome: can you offer care while allowing the recipient a life beyond your satisfaction? In the book example, you might ask about their reading without making gratitude a test. What you loved about the book can survive their response. Care has a little more room when it does not have to prove the importance of its giver.

## weil-self-centre
Teaching point: Your place in a scene is not the scene’s only point of view.
Alternatives: conversation recentered by hospital news; one town mapped from two homes; a changed plan traced to another person’s situation.
Chosen progression: Their distraction looks like a verdict about you. → You learn they are waiting for hospital news. → The same room now has another centre of concern. → You are in the scene. So is a life that does not revolve around you.
Still1: The same town can be mapped around more than one home.
Still2: A changed plan may have an independent cause.
Qualification: Her religious account goes further, connecting this surrender of imagined centrality with love and the renunciation of false divinity. That language is severe, but the ordinary distinction is approachable. A friend’s delay, a stranger’s impatience, or a changed plan may have causes beyond your role in them. Taking that possibility seriously can make a question gentler and a response more accurate. You remain present in the scene; the other people gain enough space to be present there too.

## weil-necessity
Teaching point: What happens to someone does not measure what they deserve.
Alternatives: rain falling over two morally different neighbours; worker balancing a heavy load; a repair examines cause without judging worth.
Chosen progression: Two homes stand under the same weather. → The rain follows no judgment about their owners. → A cracked roof leaks where material conditions allow it. → Find the cause. Help the person. The leak is no moral verdict.
Still1: The weight of a load responds to balance, not good intentions.
Still2: An outcome and a moral worth are different questions.
Qualification: Weil’s theological account connects necessity with a world God allows to exist beyond divine intervention. That is her interpretation, not a scientific explanation of weather or an obligation to accept avoidable harm. Knowledge of constraints can support practical action: mend the roof, improve the equipment, change the working conditions. The distinction is between understanding what produced an event and deciding that it expresses a person’s worth. Care does not have to wait for the universe to issue a verdict.

## weil-contradiction
Teaching point: A quick resolution can hide the difficulty you need to face.
Alternatives: two needs obscured by alternating slogans; a balanced beam carrying two real obligations; a schedule that marks what remains unmet.
Chosen progression: Care and exhaustion can both be real. → One slogan hides the need for rest. → Another slogan can hide the person who needs care. → Keep both facts visible. A decision can acknowledge its cost.
Still1: Both sides of a practical tension can have genuine weight.
Still2: An imperfect arrangement can leave the unmet need visible.
Qualification: Weil does not offer a universal recipe for solving incompatible duties, and her religious account should not be reduced to a decision trick. Some situations still require choosing and bearing a real loss. Attention helps preserve the seriousness of what the choice costs. It can keep an action from dressing itself up as a perfect answer. A workable compromise becomes more honest when it does not announce that the need left unmet was never important in the first place.

## weil-beauty
Teaching point: Beauty can be loved without being turned into a possession.
Alternatives: a tree observed while the camera is set down; a musical phrase that must pass to be heard; a leaf fragmented by the attempt to possess its beauty.
Chosen progression: The tree is beautiful before it becomes anything you own. → The wish to capture it can arrive almost immediately. → The camera can wait. The tree stays outside your possession. → Love of beauty can include the distance that lets it be.
Still1: A musical phrase matters through its unfolding and passing away.
Still2: Inspecting an object to own its beauty can destroy what drew us.
Qualification: This belongs to Weil’s religious understanding of the order of the world and its relation to God. It is not a rule against photographs or ownership, and beauty does not make an unjust world innocent. Her distinction concerns the movement of desire: admiration can become acquisition, but it need not. You might finish looking before taking the picture, or let a moment be valuable without collecting evidence of having had it. Something can remain dear while staying where, and as, it is.

## weil-friendship
Teaching point: Closeness needs room for two separate people.
Alternatives: two chairs approach, fuse uncomfortably, then regain room; an invitation with two legitimate answers; care that is temporarily unequal but preserves consent.
Chosen progression: Friendship brings two lives close enough to meet. → A possessive wish tries to make one life answer for both. → Distance can return without ending the connection. → Closeness protects room for an independent life.
Still1: Consent allows the same invitation to receive different answers.
Still2: Unequal help in a difficult season need not erase either person.
Qualification: She places this harmony within a demanding religious understanding of love. Everyday friendships are messier, and the balance can shift with illness, grief, or unequal need. Independence does not require perfectly equal help at every moment. The question is whether each person is still recognized as someone with a life of their own. You can say you miss a friend without making their new opportunity an offence. Tenderness has more room when the other person’s freedom is among the things you are trying to protect.

## weil-roots
Teaching point: Belonging grows through participation, memory, and a place in what comes next.
Alternatives: carpenter teaches then apprentice contributes and teaches; one person nourished by several communities; newcomer receives a meaningful shared task.
Chosen progression: A skill arrives from people who worked before you. → Shared work lets you participate in the inheritance. → Your judgment can make a contribution of its own. → The connection has a future when you can help carry it forward.
Still1: One person can belong through several living connections.
Still2: Presence becomes participation when a person has a real part in the task.
Qualification: Written during the Second World War, The Need for Roots ties this need to social reconstruction. Weil’s proposals can be debated, and roots should not be used to justify coercive tradition, exclusion, or nationalist purity. A living connection allows nourishment and growth; it need not prevent movement. The everyday question is whether a place gives someone more than a slot to occupy. Is there a history they can encounter, a responsibility they can exercise, and a future in which their contribution matters?

## weil-uprooting
Teaching point: A person can lose their place in a community without leaving the room.
Alternatives: workshop closure exposes several losses; person physically present but excluded from shared work; new roots growing after departure from harm.
Chosen progression: The workplace carries pay, skill, and a shared life. → The workshop closes. More than income disappears. → A new wage replaces one support, but the others need rebuilding. → A new connection needs participation, not merely a place to stand.
Still1: A person can remain in the room while being excluded from its shared life.
Still2: Leaving a harmful community can be necessary; nourishing roots can be rebuilt.
Qualification: Weil’s wartime analysis is historically specific, and moving away from an oppressive community can be a needed freedom. Her concept does not make staying morally superior to leaving. It asks what supports a person’s life before those supports are removed, and what would help rebuild them afterwards. A new connection may need time, shared work, language, and genuine responsibility. Welcoming someone becomes more substantial when they can become a participant, rather than remaining a permanent guest in everyone else’s story.

## weil-work
Teaching point: Obedience is different from understanding how your effort makes something.
Alternatives: bolt repeated until the whole assembly becomes visible; worker’s safety judgment stops a faulty machine; part compared with the whole it belongs to.
Chosen progression: A skilled pair of hands repeats a fragment of the task. → The finished machine and its purpose remain out of view. → The plan brings the part into relation with the whole. → Understanding matters when the worker can act on a judgment.
Still1: A fragment becomes intelligible when its place in the whole is visible.
Still2: Responsibility without authority leaves judgment unable to change the work.
Qualification: She developed these questions through political analysis and her experience of factory labour in the 1930s. This is not a claim that every repetitive task is degrading, or that better diagrams can solve exploitation. Pay, security, workload, and power still matter. The distinction helps reveal one part of the problem: a person treated only as a pair of hands has capacities the workplace refuses to use. More humane work makes understanding and practical judgment part of the worker’s actual role.

## weil-responsibility
Teaching point: Being needed means having something that is actually yours to answer for.
Alternatives: kitchen orders replaced by meaningful responsibility for late arrivals; burden distinguished from authority to respond; a learner’s real role grows through guided practice.
Chosen progression: Tiny orders can keep someone busy without giving them a part to own. → A meaningful task gives a purpose their judgment can serve. → Resources and authority let responsibility become real. → Trust includes room to act, not just a place to receive blame.
Still1: A heavy burden and meaningful responsibility differ.
Still2: Guidance can open a larger role as someone learns.
Qualification: Her account forms part of a wartime proposal for social reconstruction, not a complete theory of organizational management. Still, it offers a clear question for a workplace, family, or civic group: can this person’s judgment make a difference? Children and newcomers may need guidance; guidance can gradually open a real role. The goal is a contribution proportionate to a person’s capacities and circumstances. A community becomes more habitable when its members can help sustain it, rather than only follow orders or receive care.

## weil-obligation
Teaching point: A person’s need does not wait for them to argue their case well.
Alternatives: failed food request whose need survives the rejected form; a right beside the duties that make it effective; silent person whose need exists before advocacy.
Chosen progression: A hungry person cannot make the required case clearly. → The document fails. The human need remains. → The procedure cannot create or remove that need. → Find a response that answers to the person beyond the failed form.
Still1: A right needs others who recognize the corresponding obligation.
Still2: The need still matters when a person has no effective spokesperson.
Qualification: Weil’s argument is a contested moral foundation, not a reason to discard legal rights and protections. Rights can make obligations enforceable and help resist arbitrary charity. Her distinction keeps their purpose visible: the rule should answer to a human need, including one that has not found a persuasive spokesperson. In the queue, that might mean finding another route to support instead of treating a failed document as a completed moral decision. The procedure finishes; the person’s need may remain.

## weil-reading
Teaching point: We often react to the meaning we read into a scene.
Alternatives: watch glance reread after a train is mentioned; same expression given two context-sensitive readings; first misreading traced through a confirming reaction.
Chosen progression: A glance at a watch arrives with a meaning: boredom. → You respond to that reading. Your tone becomes cold. → A question reveals another context: their train is leaving. → The gesture stays the same. Its meaning becomes open to correction.
Still1: The same expression can be read through different backgrounds.
Still2: A first reading can produce reactions that seem to confirm it.
Qualification: This does not mean every reading is arbitrary or that genuine hostility must be doubted forever. Evidence, context, and another person’s account can make some interpretations more adequate than others. Weil connects better reading with attention and a loosening of self-centered imagination. In the watch example, you might ask whether they need to leave. The gesture stays visible, but its meaning becomes open to correction. A little distance from the first reading can change the conversation before it hardens around a mistake.
